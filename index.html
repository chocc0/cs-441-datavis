Urban Pollution in the U.S. and the Environmental Racism That Follows

CS 441 | Claire Schwartz, Miguel Chavez Nava Jr., Jhean Breard VI., Mandy Sun

Environmental cleanliness is an essential part of fostering healthy lives and creating a fair society. However, ignorance towards environmental crises has shown us the underlying struggles that result from such carelessness. Consider the Flint Water Crisis in 2015. In order to save money, the pipelines used to transport water to residents of Flint, a community that is predominately Black, were changed to ones that allowed for more lead than is safe to enter the public water supply. In 2015, more than a quarter of all Flint homes were found to have an abnormally high amount of lead in their drinking water. Flint not only suffered an environmental disaster that poisoned its population, but the event also revealed that marginalized communities bear the brunt of environmental consequences.

“Simply put, the economic situation in Flint and the logic that made providing poisoned water seem like a reasonable idea would not have occurred without racism."1 While no single person’s racial biases were to blame for the lethal levels of lead, Flint’s outcry directed national focus on “structural racism." Kemi Fuentes-George–a Middlebury College political science professor–may have synthesized it best:

"While the American political economy has done pretty well so far at externalizing environmental costs to the poor and marginalized, environmental hazards do travel, perhaps more easily than poor black citizens. In the same way that racist and authoritarian police practices have killed Eric Garner, Jack Crawford and Kelly Thomas, the environmental vulnerability that has devastated Flint – rotting infrastructure, industrial pollution, intransigent political institutions – puts us all at risk."1

For years, local officials denied claims of elevated lead concentrations and occurrences of Legionnaires Disease. The city government violated Environmental Protection Agency regulations by flushing water samples used in cleanliness reports in an effort to dismiss the allegations.2 Excess lead in Flint’s pipes reacted with chlorine, an antibacterial agent used to keep drinking water safe, and triggered an uptick in Legionnaires’ disease.3 This governmental negligence imparted elevated blood-lead levels in as many as 1 in 10 children, according to investigations at Hurley Medical Center.4 Flint’s child population is now facing developmental risks and lower life expectancies. The Flint Water Crisis demonstrates that environmental trends are difficult to track, which can result in a lack of institutional accountability for the physical consequences. Although environmental indicators point to health risks, institutions are motivated to confound and dismiss data inappropriately.

So, what happened after the Flint Water Crisis? In August 2025, the city declared the problem “fixed,” but many citizens argued as the structural racism problem had not been addressed.5 The question then becomes, what next? Or, more accurately, where is next? Where is the next city that will face infrastructural and environmental racism and have to wait over a decade for it to be “fixed?"

Let’s look at the data extrapolated from the City Health Dashboard on pollution in urban cities. Depicted are cities that have populations of over 500,000. The POC Gap Index was derived from the difference in exposure to air pollution between people of color (POC) and white residents within a city. Other statistics were directly extracted from the 2021 City Health Dashboard dataset.

Evidence shows that air pollution negatively affects us both physically and mentally. Across urban areas, there exists a positive correlation between air pollution and deaths by cardiovascular disease. Studies show that even a 7 ppb rise in ozone concentration can complicate cardiovascular health and raise blood pressure.6

However, these effects are not uniformly distributed. Marginalized groups can be seen to be experiencing outsized suffering: rural areas have higher mortality rates from cardiovascular disease, and pollution can have adverse effects on adolescent mental health and cognitive development.

We can see rural areas (between 3-10% rurality) are experiencing marginally higher cardiovascular mortality rates.7 This finding indicates that a gradient exists moving outward from urban centers to rural surroundings.

As for negative effects on adolescent cognitive development, students of color, such as Black and Hispanic student groups, are more likely to be exposed to pollution, which thus drives lower grades and higher disciplinary referrals.8 For example, in Atlanta, Black and Hispanic students experience a 13% lower high school graduation rate compared to White students.

The first step towards solving the global environmental crisis is to reflect on the current state of progress. Awareness is at an all-time high as more people recognize the consequences of environmental catastrophes, like the Flint Water Crisis. However, its recent resolution in 2025 reminds us that environmental crises can take years to fully resolve; all the while, marginalized groups may suffer the consequences.

Scientists began tracking CO2 emissions in the 1950s, but it wasn’t until the 1990s that the United Nations convened to stabilize greenhouse gas emissions. This timeline reveals a crucial flaw in our systems: the time from observation to action is dangerously long. Awareness cannot solve the problem alone. Real change must come from the institutions that shape society. Drastic action is necessary to create lasting improvements, especially as easy targets like recycling fall through the cracks. Holding politicians accountable in local governments can help to shed light on systemic environmental racism. Our power structures must regulate, restructure, and provide the means to build a cleaner, fairer future.
